01 Orientation

Why we tokenize: from characters to subwords

Neural networks cannot multiply strings. Before a Transformer can read a sentence, raw text must be converted into a sequence of discrete integers t1, t2, …, tL ∈ {0, …, |V| − 1}, each indexing a row in an embedding matrix E ∈ ℝ|V| × d.

The fundamental granularity trilemma

Every tokenizer chooses how large its units are. Historically, NLP tried two extremes before settling on subwords:

GranularityVocabulary |V|Sequence length LFatal flaw on Kwa languages
Word-level
["Mema", "wo", "akwaaba"]		 100k to 1M+ Very short (1× words) Agglutinative prefixes (mɛ-, re-, bɛ-) create millions of surface inflections; unseen words collapse into <unk>.
Character / Byte-level
['M','e','m','a',' ','w','o']		 256 (UTF-8 bytes) Very long (5× to 8× words) Transformer self-attention scales as O(L²). A 6× longer sequence costs 36× more attention compute and shrinks effective context window by 6×.
Subword-level (Leke)
["Me", "ma", "Ġwo", "Ġakwaa", "ba"]		 8k to 64k Compact (≈1.4× words) Frequent words stay intact; rarer words split into reusable pieces such as roots and affixes; unseen bytes fall back losslessly.
Total Step Cost ≈ |V| · dembedding parameters + Llayers · (Nwords · F)² · dquadratic self-attention FLOPs
|V|: vocabulary size; d: model hidden dimension; Nwords: words in document; F: fertility (tokens per word). Doubling fertility quadruples attention cost.

In Leke: The 6-stage pipeline

Leke processes every input string through a deterministic 6-stage pipeline implemented in Rust: (1) Raw UTF-8 text → (2) Unicode NFC composition → (3) Lookalike glyph harmonization → (4) Tone policy filter → (5) Byte-Level pre-tokenization & BPE merge lookup → (6) Contiguous Token IDs & byte offsets.

Check yourself

If Tokenizer A has a fertility of 4.0 tokens/word on Ewe and Tokenizer B (Leke) has a fertility of 2.0 tokens/word, how much more quadratic self-attention work (O(L²)) does Tokenizer A require on the same document?

Because self-attention scales quadratically with sequence length L = Nwords · F, halving fertility reduces the L² attention matrix by a factor of 4.

02 Part I · Information Theory & Metrics

Fertility, compression & the multilingual token tax

How do we objectively measure whether a tokenizer is good for a language? In Leke's evaluation harness (python/leke/evaluate.py), every model is judged on four intrinsic information-theoretic metrics computed on held-out test splits.

The four core evaluation metrics

Fertility = NtokensNwords     Split>2 = #{ w : |tokens(w)| > 2 }Nwords     Compression = NUTF-8 bytesNtokens     Unk% = #{ t = <unk> }Ntokens
Fertility (lower is better): average subwords per whitespace word. Excessive split rate (lower is better): share of words shattered into 3 or more pieces. Compression (higher is better): UTF-8 bytes packed per token. Unknown rate (must be 0.0%): share of tokens lost to <unk>.
Worked example: Tokenizing two Twi sentences

Fertility only means something over thousands of sentences, but two short examples show what the numbers count. Tokens below are produced by models/bpe-16k and by NLLB-200's tokenizer; the Ġ and ▁ prefixes mark a preceding space.

  • "Akwaaba ba Ghana man mu." (5 words): Leke BPE-16k gives ['A', 'kwaa', 'ba', 'Ġba', 'ĠGhana', 'Ġman', 'Ġmu', '.'] and NLLB-200 gives ['▁Ak', 'wa', 'aba', '▁ba', '▁Ghana', '▁man', '▁mu', '.']: 8 tokens each, fertility 8 / 5 = 1.60.
  • "Mɛkɔsrɛ no aduane." (3 words): Leke gives ['Mɛ', 'kɔ', 'srɛ', 'Ġno', 'Ġaduane', '.'] (6 tokens) while NLLB-200 gives ['▁Mɛ', 'kɔ', 's', 'rɛ', '▁no', '▁aduane', '.'] (7 tokens), splitting the verb into one more piece.

Reading the comparison honestly

Leke uses one 16k vocabulary for four languages, trained on curriculum text plus MADLAD-400 web text (Akan, Ewe) and Wikipedia (Twi, Ewe, Fante). On FLORES+ it beats NLLB-200 on Asante Twi (−6.3%), Akuapem Twi (−2.0%) and Ewe (−7.1%), and narrowly beats the same-size 16k Ewe-only Wikilangs tokenizer. Dedicated Twi tokenizers are still 3–5% more compact on Twi at the same vocabulary size, and larger single-language tokenizers (Wikilangs 32k, Goldfish 50k) reach lower fertility on every language they cover. On curriculum text Leke beats NLLB-200 by 28% on Ga and 21% on Fante; the only dedicated Fante tokenizer, Wikilangs Fante 16k, is 3% more compact.

Every baseline emits some <unk> tokens; Leke stays at a strict 0.0%. The Ewe-only tokenizers lose 2.5–2.7% of FLORES+ Ewe tokens to <unk>, mostly the Greek lookalike ͻ used for ɔ, which Leke normalizes.

Check yourself

Why can Leke BPE-16k match or beat NLLB-200 on Akan, Ewe, Ga, and Fante despite NLLB-200 having a 16× larger vocabulary (256,000 vs. 16,384)?

In a 200-language model, high-resource languages dominate merge priority. A focused 16,384-token vocabulary trained specifically on normalized Kwa text allocates far more merges to Kwa roots and affixes.

03 Part I · Information Theory & Metrics

Zipf's law & temperature upsampling

Even within a single language family, open datasets are deeply imbalanced. In public corpora (Wikipedia, MADLAD-400, Glot500, MGhana-ST), Akan/Twi and Ewe have roughly 6× to 8× more text than Ga and Fante. If you simply concatenate the files and train BPE, Zipf's law guarantees that Twi and Ewe monopolize the merge table.

Multinomial temperature upsampling

To prevent high-resource siblings from starving lower-resource languages, Leke's corpus pipeline (temperature_upsample in python/leke/corpus.py) re-weights each language i with raw line count Ni using a temperature exponent α ∈ [0, 1]:

pi(α) = NiαΣj=1K Njα
At α = 1.0: sampling is proportional to raw corpus size (Ga and Fante are starved).
At α = 0.0: uniform sampling (25% each; tiny corpora repeat so often that the tokenizer memorizes rare noise words).
At α = 0.5 (square-root smoothing): the default in leke.corpus. The shipped bpe-16k model uses α = 0.2, chosen on dev sets to protect Ga and Fante.
Worked example: square-root smoothing (alpha = 0.5)

Suppose our training split has Ntwi = 49,000, Newe = 36,000, Nga = 6,400, and Nfante = 4,900 lines (96,300 total):

  • Raw share (α = 1.0): Twi gets 50.9%, Ewe 37.4%, Ga 6.6%, and Fante only 5.1%.
  • Square-root weights (Ni0.5): √49,000 ≈ 221.4, √36,000 ≈ 189.7, √6,400 = 80.0, √4,900 = 70.0 (sum = 561.1).
  • Upsampled share (α = 0.5): Twi 39.5%, Ewe 33.8%, Ga 14.3%, Fante 12.5%. Ga and Fante representation more than doubles without overfitting.

Why not set α = 0.0 so all four languages get an exact 25% share during BPE training?

At α = 0.0, every sentence in a small corpus is duplicated many times, artificially inflating the count of rare proper nouns or typos in that split above common words in the larger languages.

04 Part II · Kwa Linguistics & Normalization

Unicode canonical composition (NFC vs. NFD)

Before a subword algorithm ever counts character pairs, it faces a hidden trap in Unicode: the same visible accented character can be stored in two completely different ways in UTF-8.

Precomposed (NFC) vs. Decomposed (NFD)

Consider the high-tone vowel á in Twi pápá (“goodness”):

  • Unicode NFC (Canonical Composition): Stored as a single precomposed codepoint U+00E1 (UTF-8 bytes 0xC3 0xA1).
  • Unicode NFD (Canonical Decomposition): Stored as two codepoints: base ASCII a (U+0061, byte 0x61) followed by the floating Combining Acute Accent ◌́ (U+0301, bytes 0xCC 0x81).

For open vowels like ɛ́ (U+025B + U+0301) and ɔ̀ (U+0254 + U+0300), no single precomposed Unicode point exists, so NFC keeps the base African Latin vowel followed by the canonical ordered combining mark, while composing every vowel that does have a precomposed form (á, é, í, ó, ú, ã, ẽ, ĩ, õ, ũ).

What happens if you skip NFC before BPE

In web-scraped Ewe and Twi corpora, macOS/iOS keyboards often emit NFD while Windows/Android keyboards emit NFC. An unnormalized byte-level BPE tokenizer treats NFD a + U+0301 and NFC á as unrelated byte sequences, and frequently splits the combining accent 0xCC 0x81 away from its vowel, attaching the floating tone mark to the next consonant. Leke's KwaNormalizer runs .nfc() as Stage 1 of every normalization call.

Check yourself

Why must Unicode NFC run before lookalike mapping and BPE segmentation?

NFC unifies canonically equivalent sequences into a single deterministic codepoint sequence before any table lookups or byte splits occur.

05 Part II · Kwa Linguistics & Normalization

Lookalike character harmonization

Standard QWERTY keyboards shipped in West Africa historically lacked keys for African Reference Alphabet letters (ɛ, ɔ, ɣ, ɖ, ŋ). Typists, typesetters, and OCR pipelines routinely substituted visually identical letters from Greek, Cyrillic, or IPA blocks.

The byte-level collision

To a human reader, Greek Small Letter Epsilon ε (U+03B5), Greek Lunate Epsilon ϵ (U+03F5), Cyrillic Ukrainian Ie є (U+0454), and African Latin Small Letter Open E ɛ (U+025B) look nearly identical. To a byte-level tokenizer, they share zero bytes:

Canonical Kwa Latin ɛ (U+025B):  0xC9 0x9B
Greek lookalike     ε (U+03B5):  0xCE 0xB5
Cyrillic lookalike  є (U+0454):  0xD1 0x94

06 Part II · Kwa Linguistics & Normalization

Phonemic tone & the four Kwa languages

All four target languages in Leke belong to the Kwa branch of the Niger-Congo family spoken across Ghana and Togo. They are tonal and agglutinative, meaning pitch and affixation carry core lexical and grammatical distinctions.

Why tone preservation is the strict default

Many western NLP pipelines start by stripping diacritics (unicodedata category Mn). On Kwa languages, stripping tone marks destroys meaning:

LanguageSurface Form 1Surface Form 2Surface Form 3Why stripping fails
Akan / Twi pápá (High–High)
“goodness / good”		 papá (Low–High)
“father”		 pàpà (Low–Low)
“palm-frond fan”		 All three collapse into papa, making “My father is good” ambiguous.
Ewe mí (High tone)
“we / us” (1st pl.)		 mi (Low/unmarked)
“you” (2nd pl.)		 èʋè (Low–Low)
“two / Ewe”		 Collapses first-person plural and second-person plural pronouns into the same token.

In Leke, KwaNormalizer::default() sets strip_accents = false, preserving acute (U+0301), grave (U+0300), circumflex (U+0302), tilde (U+0303), macron (U+0304), and caron (U+030C) marks. Tone stripping (strip_accents = true) is strictly opt-in for tone-insensitive keyword search.

Linguistic profile of the four supported languages

Tw · Akan / Twi (1.38 fertility)

Covers Asante and Akuapem dialects. Features 7 oral vowels (a, e, ɛ, i, o, ɔ, u), ATR vowel harmony, and rich verbal prefixing (re- progressive, bɛ- future, kɔ- motional, n-/m- negative).

Ee · Ewe / Èʋegbe (1.45 fertility)

Uses 5 extended African Reference Alphabet consonants: retroflex ɖ, bilabial fricative ƒ, velar fricative ɣ, velar nasal ŋ, and bilabial v-hook ʋ, plus nasalized vowels (ã, ẽ, ĩ, õ, ũ).

Ga · Gã (1.39 fertility)

Spoken in Greater Accra. Relies heavily on labial-velar and palatal digraphs (gb, kp, ny, ŋm, sh, ts, dz) and nasalized open vowels (ɛ̃, ɔ̃) that BPE merges into intact onset units.

Fa · Fante / Mfantse (1.34 fertility)

Coastal Akan literary variety. Shares ɛ and ɔ harmony with Twi while featuring distinct palatalized and labialized consonant clusters (dz, ts, kw, hw, tw, dw, hy).

Check yourself

When should you pass strip_accents=True to leke.normalize()?

Translation and TTS depend on phonemic tone (pápá vs. papá). Stripping tone marks is only appropriate for tone-agnostic search or retrieval against unaccented queries.

07 Part III · Subword Algorithms

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) from first principles

Introduced for data compression by Gage (1994) and adapted to NLP by Sennrich et al. (2016) and Radford et al. (2019), Byte-Level BPE builds a subword vocabulary from the bottom up: starting with individual bytes and iteratively merging the most frequent adjacent pair.

The training algorithm

  1. Initialize the base alphabet V0: Seed the vocabulary with all 256 byte symbols (0x00..0xFF) plus special tokens (<pad>, <unk>, <bos>, <eos>). Pre-tokenize text at whitespace boundaries, replacing leading spaces with the visible marker Ġ (U+0120).
  2. Count adjacent pairs: Across all words w weighted by corpus frequency f(w), count every adjacent symbol pair (u, v):
(u*, v*) = argmax(u, v) ∈ V × V   Σw ∈ Corpus f(w) · countw(u, v)
At each step t = 1, …, M, mint a new token z = u* ∘ v*, add z to V, and record the merge rule (u*, v*) → z at priority rank t.

At inference time, a word is split into base bytes and merge rules are applied in the exact priority order they were learned during training until no further pairs can be merged.

08 Part III · Subword Algorithms

Byte-Level BPE vs. Unigram Language Model

Two subword algorithms dominate modern NLP: bottom-up Byte-Level BPE (used in GPT-4, LLaMA-3, Mistral) and top-down Unigram LM (Kudo 2018, used in SentencePiece, T5, NLLB). Leke implements both in Rust (src/trainer.rs) and ran a controlled head-to-head experiment (specs/algorithm_decision.md) to decide which should be the default.

How Unigram differs from BPE

Byte-Level BPE (Bottom-Up)

Starts with 256 bytes and greedily adds the highest-frequency adjacent pair at each step until reaching |V| = 16,384.

Unigram LM (Top-Down)

Starts with a huge seed vocabulary (100k+ substrings), estimates token probabilities P(xi) via EM, and iteratively prunes tokens that least reduce corpus log-likelihood ℒ = Σ log P(x*).

Why Byte-Level BPE is the default

In Niger-Congo Kwa languages, words are formed by chaining short CV/CVC verbal prefixes (mɛ-, re-, bɛ-, kɔ-) onto stable roots, and both algorithms learn these units. Byte-Level BPE wins on three of four languages (mean fertility 1.39 vs. 1.43, with the largest gap on Ga: 1.39 vs. 1.50); Unigram is slightly better on Ewe (1.42 vs. 1.45). The deciding factor is coverage: Unigram has no byte fallback and emits stray <unk> tokens on Ewe (0.04%), while BPE stays at a strict 0.0%.

09 Part III · Subword Algorithms

The 0.0% <unk> guarantee & live tokenizer sandbox

When a character-based tokenizer encounters a character it did not see during training (an emoji, a currency sign, a mathematical operator, or a borrowed diacritic), it replaces the token with <unk>. Once <unk> is emitted, the original information is permanently destroyed: decode(encode(x)) != x.

How byte-level fallback guarantees lossless round-trips

Every valid UTF-8 string is a sequence of bytes in 0x00..0xFF. Because Leke seeds its base vocabulary with all 256 byte tokens before learning any merges, no UTF-8 string can ever be out-of-vocabulary:

  • Known Kwa words and morphemes merge into compact subword tokens (Akwaaba, Ġba, ĠGhana).
  • Unseen symbols (like the Ghana flag 🇬🇭 or math symbols ∑x² = λ) decompose into their exact UTF-8 byte tokens (e.g. <0xF0><0x9F><0x87><0xAC>), which tok.decode() reassembles losslessly back into the original characters.

Rust core, PyO3 bindings & cryptographic provenance

A tokenizer sits on the hot path of every LLM training loop and inference server. If normalization or BPE lookup runs in pure Python, CPU data loaders become the bottleneck while GPUs sit idle. Leke is written in Rust (src/) and exposed to Python via a compiled PyO3 C-extension (leke._leke).

Measured throughput (specs/benchmarks.md)

Three systems engineering choices in src/

  • Batch execution across the FFI boundary: Calling into Rust once per sentence pays Python's C-API call overhead every time. tok.encode_batch(texts) crosses the Python/Rust boundary once, normalizes and encodes the entire list inside Rust, and returns contiguous vectors.
  • ABI3 stable wheels: Configured in Cargo.toml with pyo3 = { features = ["extension-module", "abi3-py39"] }, allowing a single compiled wheel (_leke.abi3.so / .pyd) to work across Python 3.9, 3.10, 3.11, 3.12, and 3.13+ without recompiling per minor version.
  • Cryptographic provenance receipts (receipt.json): Every trained model bundle writes a JSON receipt beside tokenizer.json containing the SHA-256 hash of every training file, the exact normalizer flags, vocabulary size, and random seed (42).

11 Part IV · Systems & Library Guide

Using the Leke library: Python, Rust, Hugging Face & CLI

Now that we have covered the theory and design, here is the complete practical reference for installing and using leke in your own projects.

1. Installation & local build

From a clone of the repository using uv and maturin (requires a Rust toolchain and Python 3.9+):

# Create virtual environment and compile the native Rust PyO3 extension
uv sync
uv run maturin develop --release

# Verify the compiled C-extension version
uv run python -c "import leke; print(leke.__version__)"

2. Standalone Kwa text normalization in Python

Use leke.normalize() or the reusable KwaNormalizer class to clean raw scraped text before any NLP task:

import leke
from leke import KwaNormalizer

# Quick functional call (NFC + lookalike harmonization + preserve tone + collapse spaces)
raw = "Wↄfrε   no   pápá!   Ογε ɳkↄ ðeke."
clean = leke.normalize(raw)
print(clean)  # "Wɔfrɛ no pápá! Oɣe ŋkɔ ɖeke."

# Tone-agnostic mode (opt-in stripping of acute/grave/circumflex/caron marks)
tone_free = leke.normalize(raw, strip_accents=True, collapse_whitespace=True)
print(tone_free)  # "Wɔfrɛ no papa! Oɣe ŋkɔ ɖeke."

# Reusable Rust normalizer instance for high-throughput loops
norm = KwaNormalizer(strip_accents=False, collapse_whitespace=True)
print(norm.normalize_str("Akwaaba   ε   Ghana"))  # "Akwaaba ɛ Ghana"

3. Encoding, batching & decoding with LekeTokenizer

from leke import LekeTokenizer

# Load a trained model bundle from disk
tok = LekeTokenizer.from_file("models/bpe-16k/tokenizer.json")
print("Vocabulary size:", tok.vocab_size)

# Single-sentence encode -> returns an Encoding object (.ids, .tokens, .offsets)
enc = tok.encode("Akwaaba ba Ghana man mu.")
print("Tokens: ", enc.tokens)   # ['A', 'kwaa', 'ba', 'Ġba', 'ĠGhana', 'Ġman', 'Ġmu', '.']
print("IDs:    ", enc.ids)      # [37, 5091, 349, 562, 450, 703, 300, 18]
print("Offsets:", enc.offsets)  # [(0, 1), (1, 5), (5, 7), (7, 10), (10, 16), (16, 20), (20, 23), (23, 24)]

# Lossless round-trip decode
restored = tok.decode(enc.ids)
assert restored.strip() == "Akwaaba ba Ghana man mu."

# High-throughput batch encode & decode in Rust
batch = [
    "Wɔfrɛ me Kofi na mefiri Kumasi.",
    "Mìedó gbe na mi nyuie.",
    "Ojekoo, te oyɔɔ tɛŋŋ?",
]
encodings = tok.encode_batch(batch)
decoded_batch = tok.decode_batch([e.ids for e in encodings])

# Direct vocabulary inspection
tok_id = tok.token_to_id("Akwaaba")
tok_str = tok.id_to_token(tok_id)

4. Hugging Face transformers integration

Every Leke bundle is a standard Hugging Face tokenizer.json and converts directly into a PreTrainedTokenizerFast with <pad>, <unk>, <bos>, and <eos> configured:

from leke import LekeTokenizer
from transformers import PreTrainedTokenizerFast

# Option A: Directly from an existing LekeTokenizer instance
tok = LekeTokenizer.from_file("models/bpe-16k/tokenizer.json")
hf_tok = tok.to_transformers()

# Option B: Load directly via Hugging Face transformers
hf_tok = PreTrainedTokenizerFast(
    tokenizer_file="models/bpe-16k/tokenizer.json",
    unk_token="<unk>",
    pad_token="<pad>",
    bos_token="<bos>",
    eos_token="<eos>",
)

# Ready for PyTorch / JAX training loops with padding & attention masks
batch_tensors = hf_tok(
    ["Mema wo akye!", "Woezɔ loo!"],
    padding=True,
    truncation=True,
    return_tensors="pt",
)
print(batch_tensors["input_ids"])
print(batch_tensors["attention_mask"])

5. Using the native Rust crate (_leke)

use _leke::normalizer::KwaNormalizer;
use _leke::trainer::{train_bpe, BpeTrainingConfig, save_tokenizer_bundle};
use std::path::{Path, PathBuf};

fn main() -> Result<(), Box<dyn std::error::Error>> {
    // 1. Normalize Kwa text in native Rust
    let norm = KwaNormalizer::default();
    let cleaned = norm.normalize_str("Wↄfrε   no   pápá!");
    assert_eq!(cleaned, "Wɔfrɛ no pápá!");

    // 2. Train a Byte-Level BPE tokenizer and emit a SHA-256 provenance receipt
    let config = BpeTrainingConfig {
        vocab_size: 16384,
        min_frequency: 2,
        strip_accents: false,
        collapse_whitespace: true,
        seed: 42,
    };
    let files = vec![
        PathBuf::from("data/splits/twi/train.txt"),
        PathBuf::from("data/splits/ewe/train.txt"),
    ];
    let (tokenizer, receipt) = train_bpe(&files, &config)?;
    save_tokenizer_bundle(&tokenizer, &receipt, Path::new("models/bpe-16k"))?;
    Ok(())
}

12 Part V · Case Studies

Ten things that broke during development, and what they teach

Every design rule in Leke came from a concrete failure observed while building, training, and benchmarking the tokenizer on real West African text. Read each as a worked exercise.

1. The floating tone mark that joined the next consonant · Unicode

What happened. In decomposed (NFD) Ewe text, a vowel followed by a combining acute accent (U+0301) was segmented by byte-level BPE so that the base vowel merged with the preceding consonant while the floating accent bytes (0xCC 0x81) merged with the following consonant.

The principle. Always run Unicode NFC canonical composition before any character mapping or byte-level pre-tokenization.

Related chapter: 04 · Unicode NFC vs. NFD

2. Twin vocabulary entries for the same Twi word · Orthography

What happened. Early vocabularies contained both wɔfrɛ (Latin ɛ, 0xC9 0x9B) and wɔfrε (Greek ε, 0xCE 0xB5) as separate tokens, cutting the effective frequency of every open-vowel word in half.

The principle. Harmonize visual script lookalikes in a deterministic table (data/char_mappings.json) before both training and inference.

Related chapter: 05 · Lookalike character harmonization

3. Tone stripping that turned “father” into “goodness” · Linguistics

What happened. Enabling standard accent stripping collapsed Twi pápá (“goodness”), papá (“father”), and pàpà (“fan”) into a single token papa, and Ewe mí (“we”) into mi (“you”).

The principle. In tonal Niger-Congo languages, diacritics are phonemic, not decorative. Preserve tone by default (strip_accents = false) and make stripping strictly opt-in.

Related chapter: 06 · Phonemic tone & Kwa languages

4. Ga and Fante starved by raw corpus concatenation · Statistics

What happened. Pooling raw datasets without re-weighting gave Twi and Ewe over 88% of the training lines, leaving Ga and Fante words splintered into single characters.

The principle. Balance multi-language corpora with multinomial temperature upsampling (α = 0.5) so low-resource languages receive fair merge allocation without overfitting.

Related chapter: 03 · Zipf's law & temperature upsampling

5. Unigram shattering 64.5% of Ewe words · Algorithms

What happened. On the early 100-sentence pilot corpus, Unigram LM pruned away mid-frequency Ewe syllables containing extended Latin consonants (ɖ, ƒ, ɣ, ŋ, ʋ), pushing Ewe fertility to 3.55 vs. Byte-Level BPE's 2.77.

The principle. Test candidate subword algorithms head-to-head on held-out splits with fixed seeds before picking a default.

Related chapter: 08 · Byte-Level BPE vs. Unigram LM

6. Baseline tokenizers dropping characters into <unk> · Evaluation

What happened. SentencePiece baselines without byte fallback (NLLB-200, Wikilangs) emitted 0.02%–0.61% <unk> tokens on the held-out Kwa test splits, permanently erasing those characters.

The principle. Seed the base vocabulary with all 256 byte values so unknown characters degrade into UTF-8 bytes with a hard 0.0% <unk> rate.

Related chapter: 09 · The 0.0% <unk> guarantee

7. Non-standard exotic spaces breaking word pre-tokenization · Data hygiene

What happened. Web-scraped texts contained non-breaking spaces (U+00A0), thin spaces (U+2009), and ideographic spaces (U+3000) that standard ASCII space splitters treated as word characters, gluing whole phrases into single giant tokens.

The principle. Normalize all 15 Unicode space variants to standard ASCII U+0020 and collapse consecutive spaces in linear time.

Related chapter: 05 · Lookalike character harmonization

8. Per-sentence Python loops bottlenecking throughput · Systems

What happened. Calling Rust encode() inside a Python for loop spent more time crossing the Python/C-API boundary than running BPE merges.

The principle. Expose batch APIs (encode_batch, decode_batch) that cross the FFI boundary once and process the entire batch in native Rust.

Related chapter: 10 · Rust core & PyO3 bindings

9. Custom normalizer blocking Hugging Face AutoTokenizer · Interop

What happened. Attaching a custom Rust-only normalizer struct inside tokenizer.json caused standard transformers.PreTrainedTokenizerFast loaders (which don't link Leke's Rust crate) to reject the JSON schema.

The principle. Serialize tokenizer.json using standard composable Hugging Face normalizer primitives (NFC + Replace + PreTokenizer) so any stock transformers environment can load the bundle without custom C++ plugins.

Related chapter: 11 · Using Leke with Hugging Face

10. Unreproducible vocabulary merges across runs · Governance

What happened. Training a tokenizer twice on slightly different corpus snapshots produced subtly different token IDs, invalidating cached dataset tensors.

The principle. Emit a cryptographic receipt.json alongside every tokenizer.json recording the SHA-256 hash of every input file, config parameter, and seed.

Related chapter: 10 · Cryptographic provenance

Check yourself

Why does Leke export its normalization rules into tokenizer.json using composable standard primitives (NFC, Replace) in addition to providing the native KwaNormalizer struct?

Using standard Hugging Face normalizer primitives in the exported JSON ensures zero-friction compatibility with PyTorch, vLLM, and transformers.

Glossary

Every dotted term in the documentation, defined in one place.

ABI3 Wheel
CPython's stable Application Binary Interface (abi3-py39), allowing a single compiled Rust PyO3 wheel to run across Python 3.9 through 3.13+.
Byte-Level BPE
Byte-Pair Encoding whose base alphabet is all 256 UTF-8 byte values (0x00..0xFF), guaranteeing a 0.0% unknown-token rate on any input string.
Compression (bytes / token)
Total UTF-8 byte length of a text divided by the number of tokens produced. Higher values mean each token packs more information.
Fertility
The average number of subword tokens produced per whitespace-delimited word (tokens ÷ words). Lower fertility means shorter sequences and lower O(L²) attention cost.
Lookalike Character
A glyph from another Unicode block (such as Greek ε or γ, or Cyrillic є) typed in place of a visually identical African Latin letter (ɛ, ɣ).
Unicode NFC
Normalization Form Canonical Composition: decomposes characters and recomposes them into canonical precomposed codepoints (a + U+0301 → á).
Unicode NFD
Normalization Form Canonical Decomposition: separates precomposed accented letters into base characters plus floating combining marks.
PyO3
The Rust library and compiler bridge used to expose native Rust structs (KwaNormalizer, NativeTokenizer) as zero-overhead Python classes.
Excessive Split Rate (>2)
The percentage of whitespace words segmented into 3 or more subword tokens. Measures morphological over-fragmentation.
Temperature Upsampling
Rescaling corpus sampling probabilities by pi ∝ Niα (α = 0.5) so lower-resource languages (Ga, Fante) are not starved by larger ones (Twi, Ewe).
Unigram LM
A top-down subword algorithm that starts with a large seed vocabulary and iteratively prunes tokens that least reduce unigram log-likelihood.
<unk> Token
Special out-of-vocabulary token emitted when a tokenizer cannot represent an input character. Guaranteed 0.0% in Leke via 256-byte fallback.
